The three costs — how the engine knows a form is better ​

"How do you know the cost — and how do you know when it found a better algorithm?" The answer is three different costs at three different trust levels. Each level exists because the cheaper one can lie.

1. dag_cost — the guess ​

The e-graph holds thousands of equivalent forms; extraction picks one. dag_cost walks the program graph and sums static weights — FLOPs per op, parameter bytes, node counts. No tensors ever execute.

Why it can lie: it has no notion of memory traffic, kernel fusion, launch overhead, or cache behavior. A fused sdpa call looks expensive on paper but is fast under Inductor. Used for ranking, never for claims.

2. Measured pay — the experiment ​

A minted law cannot ship on dag_cost alone. The admission gauntlet runs the candidate on real tensors, seeded fixtures and measures wall time / peak memory. This is where "better" becomes empirical — and where 212 bad accepts died: they looked paying until measured at real sites.

Why it can still lie: fixtures are small, seeded, one GPU box — hence "regime-bounded" in the honest scope.

3. Numerical verify — the certified equality ​

After lowering, the produced module executes and its outputs are compared against the source's, fp64-exact where stated. Verification says the output is identical — nothing about speed.

"Found a better algorithm" = all three: verified-equal output + measured cheaper + ranked by the model that guided the search.

The referee separates the same way: the game's value spec (ScoringSpec) declares which axes matter; the gauntlet's measurement supplies the numbers — the two sides meet only through a plain Mapping[str, float].

One-liner: the model proposes, the fixtures measure, the verifier certifies — a law ships only if all three agree.
